Antifungal Research Using Cyanobacteria

Armillaria mellea is a highly dangerous root rot fungus affecting various tree species in New Brunswick's forests, targeting coniferous and deciduous trees, fruit trees, and perennial shrubs. It often kills 30% or more of affected, high-susceptibility hosts, especially young conifers. Moreover, in areas where Armillaria mellea outbreaks have been reported, the land cannot be used to plant other trees as the pathogen can persist in soil for decades, even without a living host. To reduce the impact of Armillaria mellea in New Brunswick's forests, I aimed to use cyanobacterial extracts, particularly the antifungal extracts of Arthrospira platensis, as a treatment for balsam fir saplings infected with the root rot disease. While certain species of cyanobacteria are toxic to humans, Arthrospira platensis in particular has been cultivated for human consumption for thousands of years, and still retains its antifungal properties, making it suitable for usage in this regard.

Through literature review, the works of Amer Morsy Abdelaziz and Mostafa M.El-Sheekh in particular guided my work. Though their work investigated the effects of cyanobacterial extracts in controlling fungal diseases found in crops, I wanted to research the effects of cyanobacteria in young conifers infected with fungal disease. As of 2025, my project is the first in the world to investigate whether the antifungal effects of cyanobacteria can be applied to trees as well. The first major challenge of this project was acquiring Armillaria mellea. Originally, I had wanted to use Armillaria ostoyae, the more aggressive cousin of Armillaria mellea. Collaborating with a researcher at the New Brunswick Museum, I submitted a request for samples of Armillaria ostoyae to be sent to me. However, the DAOMC (Canadian Collection of Fungal Cultures) did not have any strains available for use, so I pivoted to Armillaria mellea due to the similarity between the two species. I then acquired a culture of Arthrospira platensis, along with growth medium to develop subcultures.

To conduct my research, I used 4 trial groups. A control group (neither infected nor treated with the cyanobacterial extracts), a Infected-only group, a Treatment-only group, and a group that was both infected and treated. Each group contained 20 trees arranged in a cluster fashion in a pot, as Armillaria mellea typically attacks trees in forest stands, performing especially well in areas with a high tree-density. Armillaria mellea invades the root system and tree base, and can spread through soil via root-to-root contact, so arranging the trees as if they were a forest stand would improve the chances of the fungus penetrating into the root system of the trees. Once I applied the fungus/treatment, I monitored the four experimental groups for 8 weeks before finishing the experiment.

To interpret whether or not the cyanobacterial treatment had any positive effect, I measured the change in height of all the seedlings in a group compared to the beginning of the experiment, and then averaged it, since growth metrics are a key indicator of seedling health. To demonstrate that differences in change in height between groups were not due to some other factor, I also measured the change in height from when the seedlings were harvested, to when the experiment began. I also took needles from each group, and measured the change in light absorbance of the needles. Chlorophyll content in needles is another indicator of health- with more light absorbance indicating a higher level of chlorophyll within the seedlings, and lower absorbance indicating that the seedlings were in worse shape compared to the beginning of the experiment. These findings suggest that balsam fir seedlings infected with Armillaria mellea, or potentially similar root rot funguses can have the effects diminished by treatment with Arthrospira Platensis extracts, having significant implications for forestry management and plant disease mitigation strategies. Even in non-infected seedlings, receiving the treatment spray increased their light absorption, indicating that A. platensis by itself can act as a moderate fertilizer. Given the widespread impact of A. mellea and other Armillaria spp. on forest ecosystems and commercial tree plantations, the development of natural, non-toxic antifungal treatments could reduce reliance on chemical fungicides, which often have environmental and ecological drawbacks. Moreover, it could free up space that has previously been colonized by armillaria pathogens where trees were unable to be planted before.

My project later went on to the Canada-Wide Science Fair, the largest science fair hosted in Canada. There, I won a gold medal (awarded to 10 projects in each age category), the Natural Resources Challenge Award for the best project in the Natural Resources category, the Platinum award for the Best Senior project in Discovery (one of the two main categories that projects fall under, the other being Innovation), and I was selected as one of two projects to attend the Taiwan Science Fair representing Canada.
